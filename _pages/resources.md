## Helping learners reason about AI and computer science

My teaching interests center on a question: **how can we make students' reasoning visible, discussable, and open to revision?** Visual explanations, peer discussion, and thoughtful use of AI offer different ways to explore that question.

The collection below combines tools I use with resources to explore. The classroom prompts are ideas for adaptation, rather than reports of measured learning outcomes.

### Manim — make a mathematical process visible

[Manim Community](https://www.manim.community/) is a Python library for creating mathematical animations. I use Manim as one of my tools for developing visual explanations of computational concepts.

**A classroom prompt:** Pause an animation before the next transformation. Ask students to predict what will change and explain why, then compare their reasoning with the animation.

### Remotion — build explanations with code

[Remotion](https://www.remotion.dev/) supports creating videos and motion graphics with React. I use it alongside other visualization tools to make abstract processes more concrete.

**A design prompt:** Show an algorithm's input, intermediate state, and output in a consistent visual layout. Keep notation stable so learners can follow the relationship between the steps.

### CS Unplugged — explore ideas through activities

[CS Unplugged](https://www.csunplugged.org/en/) offers activities for exploring computer science without programming. It is a useful resource to explore when designing an accessible introduction to a new concept.

**A classroom prompt:** Begin with a physical or paper-based activity, then ask students to connect each action to a step in the algorithm they will implement.

### Deep Learning — connect intuition with formal ideas

The online [_Deep Learning_ textbook](https://www.deeplearningbook.org/), by Ian Goodfellow, Yoshua Bengio, and Aaron Courville, provides a reference for mathematical foundations and machine learning concepts.

**A learning prompt:** Pair a short reading with a visual explanation. Ask students to identify what the visualization captures well and what assumptions it leaves out.

## An idea that shapes my teaching

Jacques Rancière's _The Ignorant Schoolmaster_ (1987) informs my use of peer instruction. I invite students to contribute explanations, examine one another's reasoning, and take intellectual responsibility while I provide structure and disciplinary guidance.
